## Core Thesis

Most people think enterprise sales is a five-step process (intro, demo, proposal, contracting, closed won/lost). Those five "stages" are a CRM forecasting tool, not a sales process. The real motion is closer to **15 steps**, and most sellers ignore the ones that actually win deals: the follow-up intro call, the pre-demo, the post-demo debrief, the pilot move-forward process, and the post-pilot session.

The engine of the whole cycle is **alpha** — the information edge you collect from insiders that your competitors do not have. Enterprise buyers are more sensitive to the *process* you take them through than to the product demo itself. The same 15-step process closes a $100K deal and a $1M deal; the effort is identical.

## Running Case Study

Selling AI legal tech into **SpaceX's legal department** — a hard, heavily-targeted logo, a technical organization, but a non-technical buying team. Legal is used as the example because it is the largest budget line item in the enterprise (often 3–4x other budgets), it is durable, and it is risk-driven ("stomp out risk"), which is why so many legal AI startups exist.

## Usable Takeaways

- The real cycle is ~15 steps; the CRM five-stage pipeline is a weighted-forecast tool, not the path you take a buyer through. ~90% of sellers run it wrong by treating the five stages as the process.
- Enter through only two layers: the executive (chief legal officer / GC) or the N-minus-one. Never go lower — a game of telephone loses executive value and budget access.
- Use the **pincer model**: founder targets the executive, AE targets the N-minus-one, and you work to bring them together.
- Lead with **alpha** (the unfair advantage the buyer unlocks), not just a problem statement. If you can't excite an executive, you're not ready to run a top-down enterprise motion.
- The **intro call is the single most important call** — informal, no demo, no slides, no recorder, let them talk first, dig for off-the-record context. Buyers clam up on later calls once it feels like a process.
- Insert a **follow-up call** before the demo to co-author the demo with your champion; run a **pre-demo** where possible. Both are pure intel collection and competitors skip them.
- Demo only the ~20% of the product that carries 80% of the value for *these* people. Never do a cold group demo or a "present to 4–5 people" due-diligence checkbox.
- Immediately after the demo, **debrief the champion** for a raw reaction — find who might kill the deal.
- Run a tight **2–3 day pilot** with 3–4 power users (never the executive), with co-authored success criteria. Longer/integrated pilots should be paid and credited back on close.
- **Work backwards from signature before the pilot starts**: procurement, security, legal, signatory, timeline.
- Talk **pricing one-on-one with the champion post-demo**, never negotiate against yourself, and defend price via co-authorship (arm the champion with the ROI slide). Full value can land in year two on a two-year deal.
- Send **Word docs, not PDFs** (they'll redline); offer your paper or theirs; get legal on a **live call** when redlines are extensive; pick your battles.
- A healthy enterprise **win rate is ~25–35%** of qualified opportunities. Higher than that means your price is too low. ~20% pilot drop-off is normal; ~25% of lost deals boomerang within a year.
- Qualify on **change, not problem** — everyone speaks to the same problem, which commoditizes you. ~1 in 4 intro calls should disqualify on maturity.
- Expansion is the point: land, then use the founder + insider context to expand. Services are a legitimate line item — it's what enterprises know how to buy.
