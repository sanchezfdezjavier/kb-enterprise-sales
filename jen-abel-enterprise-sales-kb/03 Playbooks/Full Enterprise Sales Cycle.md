# Full Enterprise Sales Cycle

The step-by-step walkthrough of a full enterprise deal, from first meeting to signature. Source: [Video - Full Enterprise Sales Cycle 2026](<../01 Source Notes/Video - Full Enterprise Sales Cycle 2026.md>).

## Why 15 Steps, Not 5

The traditional CRM pipeline — intro, demo, proposal, contracting, closed won/lost — is a **weighted-forecast tool**, not a sales process. Each "stage" is really a bucket holding three-to-five critical meetings. Roughly 90% of founders and salespeople get this wrong by plopping buyers into their five-step process instead of **mirroring the buyer's own buying process**.

Two anchors run through every step:

- **Alpha (information edge).** The whole game is collecting insider context your competitors never get, so the buyer feels the product and the process were built specifically for them. You are always up against *something* — the status quo, a competitor, or (most often) an incumbent.
- **Same process, any deal size.** A $100K deal and a $1M deal take the same 15 steps and the same effort. Enterprises are more sensitive to the process you run than to the demo itself. Whole cycle target: ~90 days for a ~$100K deal (longer, and price accordingly, if heavy integration is required).

## Step 1 — Landing the Meeting

- Enter through **only two layers**: the executive (e.g. chief legal officer / GC / CLO) or the **N-minus-one** (a VP/director one step removed). Go any lower and the storyline becomes a game of telephone — you learn user value, not executive value, and a $100K deal needs an executive sponsor to allocate budget.
- Use the **pincer model**: the founder targets the executive; the AE / enterprise lead targets the N-minus-one. Work to pull the two together. ("I'll bring in my founder — could your boss join too?" / "Who on your team should we include?")
- The message is **2–3 sentences max**, delivered one-to-one (email, LinkedIn, cold call, event, Twitter) — executives respond to people, not marketing campaigns. The best sender to an executive is the **founder**.
- Lead with **alpha**, not just a problem. Not "we do X 10x better" (still sounds like a commodity) but "here's how to rethink your business model / restructure your team in the age of AI." Litmus test: *what would this buyer say about the product in a board meeting?*
- Assume the buyer gets hundreds of emails. Standing out is the hard part and the reason founder-led outreach matters. Iterate the message over time; use AI (OpenAI/Anthropic) to spar on who the right named targets are, then gut-check. Channel depends on the person — cold calls work for non-technical buyers, not technical ones.

## Step 2 — Running the Intro Call

The single **most important call** — buyers are at their most open here and clam up later once it feels like a process.

- Keep it **informal, ~30 min, no demo, no slides**. **Do not record it** — recording kills vulnerability.
- Open low-pressure: "I'll keep this informal, may not even need 30 minutes — let's just swap intros." **Let them go first** — every bit of context before you speak lets you frame tighter.
- Ask about change, not features: "Do you have an AI mandate? How are you doing this work today? *Does anything need to change* going into 2027 — and why now, why not wait a year?" Change is usually dictated top-down, so this surfaces executive priorities.
- Keep digging: "Off the record between us, what really matters? What do you want to own?" The deeper you dig, the more they give — but read the person (poker face vs. open) and improvise; don't pepper them.
- Spend only the **back ~10 minutes** on what you do, framed entirely around what they just shared. **No scripted pitch** — a script feels artificial and commoditizes you.
- BANT (budget, authority, need, timing) lives in the back of your mind — you never ask those questions directly.
- Vulnerability builds trust (e.g. sharing a real product limitation) — it makes the call feel less like sales.

## Step 3 — Running a Follow-Up Call (most skipped)

A short (~15 min) call **before the demo** to co-author it with your champion. Competitors skip this entirely.

- Walk the champion through the product and ask **what will resonate with the team** and **what to avoid**. Ask them to plant specific questions in the demo so value lands.
- Offer options: "Here are 5 things we could show; from our talk, these 2 matter — anything else to include so it feels holistic?"
- Decide whether to run a **pre-demo** with one more excited stakeholder first (more intel; push for it), based on how mature their decision is.
- Lock the group demo on the calendar — make it **a full hour**.

This is where you build the business case *with* them, so it feels like buying, not being sold to.

## Step 4 — Prepping the Pitch / Framing the Demo

- This is where the private thinking time goes: read between the lines of what was said, clarify interpretations with the champion ("You said X — did I read that right? Is that the priority?"). It's like prompting a human — go deeper and deeper and the answers refine.
- Know exactly **who will be in the room**, what each person cares about, what to avoid, and what a successful demo looks like *to them* — ask the champion directly ("what format does the org like — how much discussion vs. in-product?").
- **Slow down to go fast.** The whole cycle still fits ~90 days.

## Step 5 — Running the Demo

- Restart with "here's who we are and why we're doing this," fully in **their frame** — so net-new attendees feel it was built for them and lean in.
- Give net-new people airtime first ("we haven't spoken — what do you want out of today?") so they feel the love.
- **Demo the ~20% of the product that delivers 80% of the value** for these specific people. Do **not** demo everything — showing unused features lets buyers say "why pay full price for half a tool we won't use" and unravels the tight narrative. If they ask "can it also do X?", *then* reveal it ("funny you ask — we just shipped that").
- Never accept a cold "present to 4–5 people" due-diligence request — you become a checkbox demoing with zero competitive intel, and lose the deal.

Enterprise sales is tight **project management** plus tight **narrative and framing**, owning the frame to exactly who these people are.

## Step 6 — Post-Demo Debrief (most skipped)

- The moment the demo ends, text the champion: "How'd that go? Can I call you in 5 for a fresh, raw reaction?"
- Ask: where did we lose someone, where did we win, who needs more time? **There is always someone who will kill the deal** — find and protect against them.
- If someone goes quiet later, use this intel to re-engage: "Worth me grabbing 15–20 min with [person] to hear their thoughts?"

The **champion** is whoever genuinely wants this to come to life (often N-minus-one, sometimes the executive shepherding it). They will read the tea leaves and tell you when to pause.

## Steps 7–9 — Pilot: Identify → Prep → Run

- **7. Identify the move-forward process.** Control the timing — propose a **2–3 day pilot** with **3–4 power users** (never the executive — they aren't the user; but *do* include your champion). Prefer forgoing pilot fees on a short pilot to keep control of the cycle. Two pilot types:
  - **No-data pilot** (2–3 days, light lift, sandbox-style) — get value fast, shorten the cycle ~2 weeks.
  - **Integrated pilot** (1–2 months, technical, requires their data) — **charge for it and credit it back on close**. Willingness to pay is a huge signal; it's often really services anyway.
- **8. Prep the pilot.** Before anyone logs in, **work backwards from signature**: When do we sign? Can we close by end of next month? Who's the procurement lead, security lead, legal contact, signatory? **Co-author explicit success criteria and 3 specific tasks per user** — never let them just log in and meander (that puts the work back on them). Also confirm: *if the pilot succeeds, how far are they from a rollout/commitment?* If rollout is a quarter out, delay the pilot — don't burn momentum by exhausting the stages too early.
- **9. Run the pilot.** Onboard each user individually. Capture what they actually did (time in product, bugs, most-used features). Turn "I didn't like it" into intel: what wasn't clear, where did you get lost? Tighter scope = fewer people lost = more consistent experience.

Note on forward-deployed engineers: fine if they take work off the buyer's plate; a bad sign if they exist because the product is too gnarly to use. The economics don't work on a $100K deal (they suit Palantir-style multi-million deals). Often "FDE" is just an account manager title — "sparkling sales engineers."

## Step 10 — Post-Pilot Session (most skipped)

- Gather results one-on-one, via survey, or through the champion. **Mirror the actual usage data**: "We gave three tasks but most only did two — why?" so no one can retroactively claim a missing capability that nobody actually tried.
- Use the champion to nudge stalled users ("[person] logged in 15 min and didn't get past step one — can you check they're having a good experience?").
- Enterprises are structured to have **maximum friction** (deal-killers, procurement, legal, sponsor) — the champion and tight project management on the inside are your keys.
- Buyers avoid confrontation ("it's fine, it's good") — pull out the real concerns. **Going quiet is the strongest signal** something is wrong.

## Steps 11–14 — Papering & Procurement

- **11. Papering prep / set the procurement timeline.** Document the reverse-engineered timeline in an email the champion can forward to procurement: agreed pricing predicated on signing by date X, plus a **kicker/urgency reason** (e.g. a free month). Send **your paper as a Word doc** (never a PDF — they will redline) and offer: use ours, or lift into yours (theirs may be faster). Note: most enterprises use Microsoft Word, not Google Docs.
- **12. Papering review.** Redlines are guaranteed. Accept the easy ones; if redlines are extensive, get **legal on a live call** rather than endless async back-and-forth. Focus on terms that impact the business; let minor stuff go. Have your own counsel review theirs.
- **13. Papering / procurement process.** Get the procurement lead + legal on a live call for the terms you can't accept. If it's *their* paper, expect a kitchen-sink first draft (e.g. $10M liability insurance) — it's all negotiable, but **pick your battles**. The deal will not fall apart over reasonable pushback at this stage.
  - Procurement's job is **not to kill the deal** — it's to ensure the buy fits how they purchase (~30-day process). But procurement is the **only path to getting paid**: business-unit leaders can't pay you, finance pulls from budget. **Do no implementation/onboarding work until the papers are signed.** ("Stuck in procurement" is also a common excuse buyers use to avoid delivering bad news.)
- **14. Confirm the signatory** before routing for signature (sometimes it's the CFO, not the sponsor) so any delay gets pinged to the right person.

## Step 15 — Signature

The exciting part. Just make sure the internal client knows who the signatory is so nothing stalls at the finish line.

Then: you're only as good as your pipeline — on to the next.

## Pricing Inside the Cycle

- Hold pricing until **post-demo**, discussed **one-on-one with the champion** (avoids sticker shock from people who missed the value-building). If pushed earlier, give a ballpark range ("somewhere between 150 and 250, with dependencies").
- **Arm the champion to defend the price**: "Where will you cut? How will you defend it? Can I build the ROI slide for you?" Let them tell you how to make it work.
- Three responses to a price: (1) all aligned, (2) not concerned, (3) "not sure I can go to bat for this." On (3): "Let's work on it together — what can you go to bat for?" — never say "discount," never throw out numbers, **never negotiate against yourself** (they may ask for a $20K cut when you'd have gone lower). Offer a **year-two step-up to full value on a two-year deal**.

## Benchmarks

- Healthy enterprise **win rate: ~25–35%** of qualified opportunities. Higher means **your price is too low**. The market talks — never charge a friend 50 and someone else 500.
- Rough funnel: ~50–75% of well-targeted outreach → qualified lead; ~half of those → demo; ~a quarter advance beyond. Bottom-of-funnel loses little if qualification was good.
- **Pilot success ~80% (only ~20% drop-off).** Below that, your pilot or qualification is off — or you're over-convincing on a product that isn't ready.
- ~1 in 4 intro calls should disqualify on maturity. ~25% of *lost* deals boomerang within a year.
