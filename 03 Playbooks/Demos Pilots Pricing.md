# Demos Pilots Pricing

## Demo Rule

Do not demo the whole product. Jen's repeated pattern is that most value comes from a small part of the product, but founders often flatten the sale by showing everything.

Better demo:

- Use the buyer's context.
- Tell a tight story.
- Show the specific workflow that changes the buyer's world.
- Let the buyer feel the before-and-after.
- Skip generic collateral when three precise bullets or an in-product walkthrough would do.

## The 80/20 Demo

Demo the ~20% of the product that carries 80% of the value **for these specific people** — learned from the earlier calls. Showing everything invites "why pay full price for half a tool we won't use" and unravels the tight narrative. If they ask "can it also do X?", *then* reveal it ("funny you ask — we just shipped that").

## Two Calls That Precede The Demo (both skipped by competitors)

- **Follow-up call (~15 min)** — co-author the demo with your champion: what will resonate, what to avoid, which questions they'll plant so value lands. Offer "here are 5 things; these 2 matter — anything to add?" Lock the group demo as a **full hour**.
- **Pre-demo** — where possible, run one with an additional excited stakeholder first. Pure intel; push for it.

## Group Demo Warning

A group demo too early can kill a deal. It pulls the conversation away from discovery and turns it into theater before the seller knows the internal narrative. Never accept a cold "present to 4–5 people" due-diligence request — you become a checkbox demoing with zero competitive intel.

## Post-Demo Debrief (skipped by most)

The moment the demo ends, text the champion for a raw reaction: where did we lose someone, where did we win, who needs more time? **There is always someone who will kill the deal** — find and protect against them. Later silence is the strongest warning sign.

## Pilot Rule

Pilots are late-stage. Treat them as bottom-of-funnel proof after the buying process has real shape, not as a middle-funnel discovery mechanism. Smart buyers always want one — build it into the process.

- Prefer a **tight 2–3 day pilot** with **3–4 power users** (never the executive — they aren't the user; but *do* include your champion). Forgo pilot fees on short pilots to keep control of the cycle.
- **Co-author explicit success criteria and ~3 specific tasks per user** — never let them just log in and meander (that puts the work back on them). Onboard each user individually.
- Longer/integrated pilots (1–2 months, needs their data): **charge and credit it back on close** — willingness to pay is a huge signal, and it's often really services.
- **Before the pilot starts, work backwards from signature**: sign-by date, procurement lead, security lead, legal, signatory. Also confirm how far a successful pilot is from rollout — if rollout is a quarter out, delay the pilot rather than burn momentum.
- **Post-pilot session** (skipped by most): mirror actual usage data ("we gave 3 tasks, most did 2 — why?") so no one claims a missing capability nobody tried; use the champion to nudge stalled users.

## Pricing Rule

Do not share pricing before understanding:

- How the purchase will be funded.
- Who must defend it.
- Whether the buyer believes the value is big enough.
- What internal category the vendor will fall into.

Jen's 2025 podcast discussion also warns against low enterprise pricing that is too big for self-serve but too small to create real executive attention.

## When To Talk Price

Hold pricing until **post-demo**, discussed **one-on-one with the champion** (avoids sticker shock from people who missed the value-building). If pushed earlier, give a ballpark range ("somewhere between 150 and 250, with dependencies"). Price should track the sales cycle — ~$100K for a 90-day cycle; $250–300K if it takes ~9 months / heavy integration.

## Defend Price Through The Champion

Arm the champion to defend the number: "Where will you cut? How will you defend it? Can I build the ROI slide for you?" Three responses to a price: (1) all aligned, (2) not concerned, (3) "not sure I can go to bat for this." On (3): "let's work on it together — what can you go to bat for?" — never say "discount," never throw out numbers, and **never negotiate against yourself** (they may ask for a $20K cut when you'd have gone lower). Offer a **year-two step-up to full value on a two-year deal**.

## Discounting

Enterprise buyers often do not want a discount as much as they want a harder-working solution. If value and price feel mismatched, the stronger move may be to increase the value case rather than cut price. The market talks — never charge a friend 50 and someone else 500.
